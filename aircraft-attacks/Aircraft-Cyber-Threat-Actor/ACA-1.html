1.Executive summary

BoxStrike assesses that ACA-1 is a Russia-linked aircraft cyber threat actor whose activity evolved between 2023 and 2026 from spoofing-dominant interference into a broader hybrid profile combining spoofing, jamming, and wider signal-pressure behavior. The campaign appears to have been shaped by both tactical needs and strategic signaling, especially in regions where civil aviation, military interest, and geopolitical tension overlap.

The report is based on open-source intelligence, researcher-collected material, supporting news coverage, and technical observation of incidents and distribution infrastructure. It does not attempt to present every claim as absolute fact. Instead, it distinguishes between observed behavior, researcher assessment, and analytical interpretation. That separation matters because aircraft cyber attribution is rarely clean.

2.Methodology and confidence framing

This report uses a threat-intelligence style of writing rather than a purely academic tone. That means the content is organized around observed incidents, actor behavior, operational context, and confidence framing. Where the evidence is strongest, the report says so. Where the evidence is inferential, the report says that too.

BoxStrike's ACA framework treats repeated behavior, infrastructure reuse, timing, regional clustering, and target selection as important indicators. In practice, no single indicator is enough on its own. What makes the analysis useful is the combination of signals across time.

Analyst note In a report like this, wording matters. "Assessed," "likely," "appears," and "high confidence" are not soft language; they are the standard way to keep attribution proportional to the evidence.

3.ACA-1 identity and strategic environment

ACA-1 is the codename BoxStrike uses for an assessed aircraft cyber threat actor linked to Russia. The actor's behavior is associated primarily with GPS spoofing, GPS jamming, and electronic warfare activity affecting aircraft navigation and adjacent communications environments.

The geographic pattern is central to the assessment. The activity clusters around places that make strategic sense: Kaliningrad and the Baltic region, the Black Sea and Ankara FIR, Smolensk as an internal test environment, the Iraq-Iran corridor, and the United Kingdom-linked aviation space.

"ACA-1" is BoxStrike's own designation, consistent with standard threat-intelligence naming practice (comparable to UNC/APT/TA-style labels used by other vendors). BoxStrike also tracks the actor's regional sub-clusters informally as Kaliningrad Group, Black Sea Hunters, and Sevastopol Team — internal working aliases, not externally reported names. Ground infrastructure associated with the Kaliningrad cluster includes the mobile GT-01 Murmansk-BN antenna trucks identified by Polish researchers and the Borisoglebsk-2 electronic-warfare system referenced in reporting on the December 2023 Baltic Fleet exercises Reported.

4.Targeting logic and aircraft selection

The report argues that ACA-1 does not select aircraft at random. Instead, the target set appears to favor aircraft whose navigation stack depends heavily on the trustworthiness of GNSS inputs, especially aircraft with integrated FMS and IRS behavior. Honeywell Primus Epic, Collins Pro Line Fusion, and similar platforms are discussed because they create a useful environment for a signal-manipulation actor.

Flight profile matters as much as avionics type. Long-haul routes, oceanic sectors, conflict-adjacent corridors, and regions with reduced independent verification make signal manipulation more useful to an attacker. This reading is consistent with independent aviation-safety reporting, which has separately noted that GPS spoofing near conflict zones increasingly "captures" integrated avionics suites — affecting GPS, inertial reference, and autopilot systems together rather than GPS alone.

The report's core point is not that every aircraft is attacked equally. It is that ACA-1 seems to choose aircraft where navigation dependence, regional sensitivity, and public visibility intersect.

4.1 Why gradual manipulation defeats built-in cross-checks (plausibility-filter logic)

Modern integrated navigation avionics do not trust GPS blindly. A Hybrid IRS continuously cross-checks incoming GPS position against its own self-contained inertial solution (derived from gyroscopes and accelerometers) and is designed to reject a GPS input that disagrees with the inertial trajectory by an implausible amount. Honeywell has publicly stated that its LASEREF VI hybrid IRS-GPS solution rejects a spoofed GPS position shift of up to 3 nautical miles over a six-minute window, and up to 60 nautical miles over 60 minutes, before falling back to inertial-only navigation; Honeywell has said newer algorithms tighten that further, to reject shifts as small as 0.2 NM sustained over six minutes.

This is the structural reason the incidents in Sections 5 and 6 are analytically significant: for a spoofed position to be accepted by the IRS-GPS fusion logic rather than rejected outright, the false position has to diverge from the aircraft's true track gradually — within the plausibility envelope the system is designed to tolerate — rather than jumping instantly to a distant false location. Aviation Week's October 2023 reporting on the UM688 corridor incidents describes exactly this mechanism from the failure side: once the IRS accepts a sequence of GPS updates that stay inside its plausibility bounds, later comparisons against ground-based navigation aids can no longer resolve the resulting large cumulative error, and the IRS itself can become unusable — a design limitation Honeywell has separately acknowledged, noting current-generation IRS was "not designed to counter this type of attack" at the time of the 2023 Middle East incidents.

This same logic explains why the Smolensk "circle spoofing" pattern (Section 5.3) is analytically distinct from a blunt jamming attack: a slowly-rotating false position that stays within a roughly one-kilometer radius is, by construction, the kind of small, continuous drift that plausibility filters are least equipped to flag — consistent with Stanford GPS Lab's observation that the circular pattern may also be intended to keep the reported Horizontal Position Error small enough to avoid triggering onboard alerts.

4.2 Independently published detection indicators

The following indicators are drawn from aviation-safety publications and manufacturer guidance aimed at helping crews recognize an ongoing spoofing event — they describe symptoms to watch for, not a method for causing them:

  • A sudden or unexplained increase in Estimated Position Uncertainty (EPU) on the navigation display.
  • An incorrect UTC time readout, since GPS-derived timing can be manipulated along with position.
  • A large, discontinuous jump in displayed GPS position relative to the aircraft's known track.
  • Navigation-page or PFD warnings flagging position-error or navigation-integrity faults.
  • Other aircraft in the same area independently reporting comparable position errors.
  • A Navigation Integrity Category (NIC) or Navigation Accuracy Category for position (NACp) value inconsistent with the containment radius normally expected in cruise flight (per ADS-B equipment standards, a NIC of 7 corresponds to a 370-meter containment radius — far tighter than the multi-kilometer errors seen in confirmed spoofing events).
  • An implausibly low ground speed derived from successive position reports — Stanford GPS Lab research notes the Smolensk circular pattern corresponded to an apparent ~200-knot speed, well below normal cruise speed for the aircraft type, which is itself a usable detection signal.

Sources: Honeywell Aerospace ("Inertial Reference Systems and GPS Spoofing," and "Spoofing and Jamming" blog); Aviation Week, "The Serious Threat of GPS Spoofing" (Oct 2023); Stanford GPS Lab (Lo et al., ION ITM 2025–2026 papers); OpsGroup Risk Warning (Sep–Nov 2023).

4.4 How GNSS spoofing signals are generally constructed (published taxonomy)

Academic literature on GNSS spoofing — most of it originating from the same University of Texas at Austin group (Humphreys, Psiaki, Shepard, Bhatti) that identified the Smolensk source in Section 5.3 — has publicly classified spoofing attacks into a small number of categories for over a decade. Jamming is the simplest and is distinct from spoofing: it floods the receiver with broadband noise so no position can be computed at all, rather than substituting a false one. True spoofing falls broadly into two families. Meaconing intercepts a genuine GNSS signal and rebroadcasts it with added delay and higher power, causing the receiver to compute a position based on the meaconer's location rather than its own; this is the oldest known form, dating to WWII-era radio-navigation deception, and requires no ability to synthesize the underlying signal structure. Generated (simulated) spoofing instead synthesizes a self-consistent GNSS signal from a receiver-spoofer device; Humphreys' own 2008 demonstration showed such a device can first overlay its false signal on top of the genuine one, gradually raise its power until it captures the victim receiver's tracking loop, and only then "drag" the computed position away from truth in small, continuous steps — which is precisely the gradual-drift behavior that the plausibility filters described in Section 4.1 are designed, with limited success, to catch.

None of the open-source reporting consulted for this report discloses which of these techniques ACA-1-linked infrastructure specifically uses for the incidents in Sections 5 and 6, and this report does not speculate on that point. The categories above are provided as general, long-published background for readers unfamiliar with the field, not as a technical description of any single incident.

5.Observed activity in 2023

5.1 Iraq-Iran border / UM688 corridor — Confirmed

In September 2023, at least 20 aircraft were reported affected by a GPS spoofing campaign in the UM688 corridor near the Iraq-Iran border; industry monitoring group OpsGroup had logged 20 separate incident reports by 28 September 2023. Aviation Week's independent reporting on the same events lists an Embraer Legacy 650, a Challenger CL650, a Dassault Falcon 8X, and a Bombardier Global Express among the affected aircraft, with at least one aircraft coming close to entering Iranian airspace without clearance. Some aircraft in the affected set experienced FMS, IRS and autopilot disruption consistent with the spoofed signal being accepted into the navigation chain rather than merely producing a loss of signal.

5.2 Ankara FIR / Bombardier Global Express 7500 — Confirmed

In a November 2023 incident reported by pilot-information service OpsGroup and German aviation outlet aero.de, a Bombardier Global Express operating from Adana to Larnaca had its GPS receivers spoofed to show a false position at Beirut's airport (OLBA). The crew disabled the aircraft's GPS receivers as a precaution after recognizing the false position on the navigation display.

5.3 Smolensk / circle spoofing — Confirmed

Beginning around 25 December 2023, aircraft over the Smolensk region of Russia were spoofed into flying an apparent circular ground track. The source location was identified by University of Texas at Austin researcher Zach Clements — working with Professor Todd Humphreys' Radionavigation Laboratory — as a field roughly one kilometer from Russia's decommissioned Smolensk military airbase. Independent GNSS-monitoring research (Stanford's GPS Lab) subsequently confirmed the pattern and noted that the interference affected GPS, inertial reference and autopilot behavior together, describing the daily reach as "tens if not hundreds" of commercial flights. Ukrainian media reporting cited by aviation outlets linked the timing to a mid-December 2023 Russian Baltic Fleet electronic-warfare exercise in Kaliningrad using the Borisoglebsk-2 system.

6.Observed activity in 2026

6.1 Kaliningrad infrastructure expansion — Confirmed

On 26 May 2026, Lithuania's Communications Regulatory Authority (RRT) told Reuters that Russian GPS-spoofing antenna infrastructure in Kaliningrad had grown from three antennas in early 2025 to 36 by May 2026 — a twelvefold increase — with an effective spoofing radius of roughly 450 kilometers, enough to reach Estonia, Latvia, Lithuania, Poland, Finland, Sweden, Belarus and the Baltic Sea. RRT deputy head Darius Kuliešius described the interference as "systemic, constant, and endless." The regulator also reported degraded mobile-network quality near Kaliningrad and disruption to GPS-dependent public transport tracking in Klaipėda during interference spikes, and said spoofing/jamming intensity correlates with Ukrainian drone strikes on Russian territory.

6.2 UK Defence Secretary aircraft — Confirmed (incident); Reported (Russia attribution)

On 21 May 2026, an RAF Dassault Falcon 900LX (an Envoy IV CC.1, registration G-ZABH, operated by Centreline AV Limited on behalf of 32 (The Royal) Squadron at RAF Northolt) carrying UK Defence Secretary John Healey had its GPS signal jammed for the duration of a roughly three-hour return flight from Estonia, with onboard phones and laptops also losing internet connectivity; pilots used alternative navigation methods. The incident was first reported by The Times and confirmed by the UK Ministry of Defence, which called it "reckless Russian interference" via a defense source, though the MOD did not formally confirm deliberate targeting or issue an official state attribution naming Russia. A broadly similar jamming incident affected a previous Defence Secretary's RAF aircraft near Kaliningrad in March 2024.

6.3 British commercial aviation disruption — Reported, with one figure corrected

Sustained GPS jamming and spoofing affecting UK-linked commercial carriers has been documented since at least 2023–2024: analysis of flight logs via GPSJAM.org reported by The Sun found 2,309 Ryanair flights, 1,368 Wizz Air flights, 82 British Airways flights and 4 easyJet flights affected by suspected Russian interference between August 2023 and the end of March 2024 — roughly 46,000 total navigation-problem reports over the Baltic region in that period. Coverage from 2026 (RTE/BBC, Aviation Week, CNN) confirms the disruption has continued and intensified through 2025–2026, including reports of GPS spoofing "capturing" aircraft avionics suites more broadly, but a specific figure of 160 UK aircraft affected in a single day on 21 May 2026 could not be located in any independent source.

8.Tactical evolution over time

Viewed together, the confirmed incidents in this report support a real qualitative trend: 2023 activity (UM688, Ankara FIR, Smolensk) is spoofing-dominant; 2025–2026 activity (Kaliningrad expansion, the Healey incident, sustained UK-linked disruption) shows a more hybrid spoofing-and-jamming posture and a much larger infrastructure footprint. Independent aviation-safety sources (ICAO North Atlantic operations bulletins, Aviation Week, EASA/IATA joint statements) separately corroborate that GNSS interference affecting commercial aviation has grown broader and more severe since 2022, consistent with the direction — though not every specific number — in this report's earlier drafts.

9.Analytical interpretation

BoxStrike assesses that ACA-1-linked activity plausibly serves multiple purposes at once: air-defense coverage against Ukrainian drones (a motive Russian authorities themselves have offered), deterrence signaling toward NATO members, and pressure on civil aviation confidence. Independent reporting (AP, "What to know about Russia's GPS jamming operation in Europe") notes that Russian authorities frame the jamming as defensive, protecting cities and military sites from drone attack, while Baltic officials describe the interference as extending far beyond what a purely defensive posture would require.

The report does not force every incident into the same motive. Repeated interference changes expectations: airlines plan around uncertainty, crews grow more alert to false-navigation conditions, and regulators increasingly treat GNSS integrity as a recurring rather than exceptional concern — a shift documented independently by ICAO, EASA, and IATA statements referenced in Section 11.

10.Conclusion

Even after removing figures that could not be independently verified, the confirmed record supports BoxStrike's core conclusion: a pattern of GPS spoofing and jamming activity assessed as Russia-linked has affected civil and military aviation from 2023 through 2026, infrastructure has demonstrably expanded (documented antenna growth in Kaliningrad), and named high-profile incidents (the November 2023 Ankara FIR case, the December 2023 Smolensk circle-spoofing pattern, the May 2026 Healey aircraft incident) are independently corroborated.
