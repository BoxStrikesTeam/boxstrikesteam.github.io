1.Executive summary

BoxStrike assesses the GNSS jamming and spoofing activity of ACA-3, an actor it evaluates as China-linked, over the 2023–2026 period. The activity shows a different dynamic from the Russia-linked ACA-1 and Israel-linked ACA-2: rather than a systematic, land-based campaign, it is a mixed pattern of military-operation byproducts (South China Sea, Taiwan Strait), sovereignty signaling, and civilian-sourced interference from unauthorized equipment (Shanghai, Xiamen).

This report has been cross-checked against independently published sources. Most of the incident record in the earlier draft was broadly corroborated; the most important correction concerns "circle spoofing," which independent sources place in China as early as 2019 rather than first appearing in 2024 — see Sections 6.2 and 13.3.

2.Introduction and methodology

This report examines GNSS interference activity assessed as China-linked using open-source intelligence methods. Sources include aviation-safety publications, academic research, international news agencies, and official agency reports (FAA, ICAO).

3.ACA-3 identity and strategic environment

"ACA-3" is BoxStrike's own designation for the aircraft cyber threat actor it assesses as China-linked. Its activity is concentrated in GPS jamming, GPS spoofing, VHF interference, and electronic-warfare categories.

3.1 Strategic context

ACA-3's activity is associated with China's expanding military capabilities and regional sovereignty claims: military presence in the South China Sea, tension in the Taiwan Strait, and warship operations off Australia's coast. The Shanghai and Xiamen incidents also show that interference is not always state/military-sourced — it can originate from unauthorized civilian equipment, and is treated separately in the sections below.

4.Targeting logic and aircraft selection

ACA-3's target selection shows dynamics distinct from other actors: rather than a systematic, land-based campaign, it is a mix of geographic location (South China Sea, Taiwan Strait, waters off Australia), the byproduct of military exercises, and incidents traceable to unauthorized civilian equipment (Shanghai, Xiamen).

5.Observed activity in 2023

5.1 Qantas aircraft and Chinese warships (March 2023) — Confirmed

In March 2023, Qantas issued new standing operating orders informing pilots of VHF interference in the Western Pacific and South China Sea from stations claiming to represent the Chinese military, and of GPS jamming suspected to originate from warships operating off the northwest shelf of Australia. IFALPA confirmed that some airline and military aircraft had been called over 121.50 or 123.45 MHz by warships, and voiced concern that GNSS/radio-altimeter systems might also be subject to interference. Qantas stated no safety events had been reported.

5.2 Shanghai Pudong Airport GPS interference — Confirmed (general pattern)

According to the Shanghai Municipal Commission of Economy and Information Technology's 2023 year-end radio-management review, Pudong Airport experienced repeated GPS signal interference during the year, and Hongqiao Airport's weather radar was also affected by an unidentified signal; authorities located and seized unauthorized GPS jammers and irregularly operated radio transmitters. The earlier draft's exact date ("17 March 2023"), the "excavation truck" detail, and the "unsafe go-around" detail could not be corroborated by a second independent source during this revision and were found only consistent with the general annual summary report.

5.3 Rizhao Airport GPS interference — Reported

The Rizhao Airport incident cited in the earlier draft rests on a complaint filed with the Shandong Provincial Bureau of Industry and Information Technology. A second, independent source could not be cross-verified during this revision; the claim is plausible and consistent with the general Shanghai pattern, but relies on a single source only.

5.4 GPS interference over Taipei (April 2023) — Confirmed, absent from the earlier draft

Between 21–25 April 2023, GNSS/GPS interference was observed over Taipei for the first time, on four consecutive days. Taiwan's National Communications Commission (NCC) dispatched a radio-detection vehicle following a complaint from the Taoyuan Air Station Tower and found intermittent interference on an adjacent frequency band; whether the source was overflow from Taiwan's own public-authority transmitters or another source was not publicly clarified. This incident has been added to ACA-3's geographic clustering table (Taiwan Strait, see Section 3.2) as an early example.

6.Observed activity in 2024

6.1 First aviation spoofing detected near Hong Kong and Tokyo (5 July 2024) — Confirmed

On 5 July 2024, SkAI Data Services announced it had detected aircraft spoofing near Hong Kong and Tokyo — the first time either major city had seen this activity (it had previously been observed elsewhere in China and in Korea). SkAI noted it could not identify a clear motivation for activity in this area, unlike the Baltic (Russian objections to NATO weapons deployments) or Korea (military exercises).

6.2 "Circle spoofing" — date correction required — Confirmed (phenomenon), date incorrect

Verification note — date correction The earlier draft stated that "circle spoofing" was first observed in China in 2024. Independent sources (C4ADS, SkyTruth, MIT Technology Review, GPS World — November/December 2019) show the technique was actually documented in 2019, at more than 20 locations along the Chinese coast (16 of them oil terminals); UT Austin's Todd Humphreys also contributed to the analysis at the time. The motivation theories cited in the earlier draft (concealing illegal sand dredging from the Yangtze River, evading U.S. sanctions on Iranian oil) are accurate, but this has been a known phenomenon since 2019. The 2024 SkAI detection concerns the pattern's first appearance in aviation (ADS-B) data — it was documented in maritime (AIS) data much earlier. This revision clarifies that distinction.

7.Observed activity in 2025

7.1 Taiwan Strait military exercises and GPS interference — Reported

The earlier draft's claim that civilian pilots reported GPS signal fluctuations during China's 2025 exercises around Taiwan could not be corroborated by a second independent source during this revision. The general pattern — GNSS interference coinciding with Taiwan Strait military exercises — is consistent with the 2023 Taipei incident (see Section 5.4) and with regional jamming maps.

7.2 PLA electronic-warfare claim — Reported

The claim that PLA electronic-warfare units deceived a foreign reconnaissance aircraft in the South China Sea with false radar signals from 300 km away, as the earlier draft notes, originates from a Chinese military magazine. No independent Western source could be cross-verified during this revision; such claims occasionally appear in PLA-adjacent publications but their verifiability remains limited.

8.Observed activity in 2026

8.1 Mass spoofing event around Beijing — Reported

The general phenomenon of GPSwise/SkAI detecting spoofing near Beijing is consistent with multiple sources published in 2026 (AVSEC Pro, NBAA). The earlier draft's exact date ("20 January 2026") and exact figure ("more than 70 flights") could not be corroborated by a second independent source during this revision and are marked "Reported."

8.2 Xiamen Gaoqi Airport GPS outage — Reported

The incident attributed to the Xiamen Radio Administration Bureau in the earlier draft rests on a single regional source; a second independent source could not be verified during this revision. It has been treated as plausible given consistency with the similar pattern in Shanghai (see Section 5.2).

8.3 Beijing listed in the FAA's official guide — Confirmed

In the updated version (Version 1.1) of its GPS/GNSS Interference Resource Guide, published in March 2026, the FAA listed the Eastern Mediterranean, the Black Sea, Russia and the Baltic region, the India/Pakistan border, Iraq and Iran, North and South Korea, and the Beijing area among the world's regions most affected by spoofing. This is corroborated by multiple independent sources including Aviation Week, GPS World, NBAA, and JDA Solutions.

11.2 "Circle spoofing" and illicit activity

The "circle spoofing" technique observed in China shows this technology is not used for military purposes alone. Motivations such as concealing illegal sand dredging and hiding oil shipments point to its use in economic and commercial domains as well (see Section 6.2 for the date correction).

11.3 Civilian-infrastructure-sourced interference

The Shanghai and Xiamen incidents show that GPS interference is not always state- or military-sourced. Unauthorized GPS jammers installed on private vehicles can pose a serious safety risk to civil aviation.

11.4 Geopolitical context

China's GPS interference activity should be assessed within a broader geopolitical context: sovereignty claims and military presence in the South China Sea have made GNSS interference a strategic tool; rising tension in the Taiwan Strait has raised concerns that this capability could be used in a potential conflict scenario; interference affecting Qantas aircraft has been interpreted by some commentators as a response to Australia's AUKUS submarine agreement (this is a contemporaneous interpretation reported in the press, not an established causal finding).

12.Conclusion

The verifiable record supports the finding that traces of ACA-3's GNSS interference are consistently present around the South China Sea, the Taiwan Strait, and major Chinese airports from 2023 to 2026, but with a more mixed motivational profile than the Russia- and Israel-linked actors — military byproduct, sovereignty signaling, civilian unauthorized equipment, and possible concealment of illicit commercial activity. This revision's most significant correction concerns the origin of "circle spoofing," which is 2019 rather than 2024; beyond that, most of the incident record was either corroborated or found plausible on a single source.

Limitations This document is based on BoxStrike's open-source research and has been cross-checked against independent sources during this revision.
