1. Executive Summary

Operation Glasscache — Boxstrike TeamTLP: WHITE

Boxstrike Team has spent the past several weeks tracking a cluster of commodity infostealer distribution activity that shares a distinctive and consistent infection vector: the abuse of Google's own trusted web infrastructure — specifically sites.google.com and drive.google.com — to host and distribute malicious payloads disguised as video-game cheats, "mod menus", and pirated or "free" software.

We have named this activity Operation Glasscache: "Glass" for the transparent, trusted veneer that a well-known and rarely-blocked domain like Google's lends to otherwise unremarkable malware, and "cache" both as a nod to the operation's original working name and to the attackers' practice of stashing payloads inside Google's own storage and hosting products rather than dedicated, more easily blocklisted infrastructure.

During this investigation, Boxstrike Team performed in-depth static and dynamic analysis of two distinct malware families distributed through this vector:

  • ScarfaceStealer (sample KiddonsModMenu.exe) — a heavily packed, self-signed trojan disguised as a game "mod menu", using direct syscalls and process-memory hooking to evade EDR detection.
  • Silent Stealer, delivered through a loader built with the ShinySpider NSIS/Electron wrapper (sample Setup.exe) — an Electron-based dropper impersonating a Razer Inc. utility, which systematically disables Windows Defender protections, exfiltrates via Telegram, and stages a secondary payload.exe.

Of the two, the ScarfaceStealer / mod-menu lure family was, in our observation set, the malware most consistently distributed directly through Google's own web properties, while the Silent Stealer / fake-installer family was the second most frequently encountered payload in our sample collection.

Assessment HIGH CONFIDENCE
The operation(s) behind this activity are financially motivated, commodity cybercrime actors — almost certainly customers or affiliates of Malware-as-a-Service (MaaS) offerings advertised on Telegram — rather than a single cohesive APT group. The common thread is not shared authorship of the malware, but a shared, deliberate choice of distribution infrastructure that abuses the reputation of Google's domains to bypass web filtering, browser warnings, and blocklist-based defenses.

2. Operation Background & Naming

Operation Glasscache — Boxstrike TeamTLP: WHITE

2.1 Why we started this investigation

Boxstrike Team's investigation began with the static and dynamic triage of a single suspicious executable, KiddonsModMenu.exe, submitted for analysis on the assumption that it was a pirated cheat tool for a video game. Structural analysis of the binary — mismatched internal metadata, a self-signed Authenticode certificate with an implausible "Not Before" date, obfuscated section names, and an entry point protected by anti-disassembly stub code — indicated deliberate concealment inconsistent with legitimate software.

Cross-referencing the file hash against sandbox telemetry (Recorded Future Triage, VirusTotal) confirmed the sample as belonging to the ScarfaceStealer family. Pivoting on distribution artifacts and infrastructure overlaps led the team to a second, unrelated-at-first-glance sample — a fake "Razer" installer, Setup.exe — which on inspection turned out to be an Electron-packaged dropper for a Telegram-operated "Silent Stealer" payload, wrapped by a custom NSIS builder we could fingerprint via a uniquely named plugin, SpiderBanner.dll.

Both samples, and a wider set of related hashes gathered during the investigation (Section 10), trace back to download pages hosted directly on Google's own domains.

2.2 Operation naming

The investigation was internally referred to during its early stages as "Operation Önbellek" ("Operation Cache" in Turkish), reflecting the team's original framing of the work as "clearing out" abuse of Google's caching and hosting infrastructure. For this public-facing report, Boxstrike Team has renamed the effort Operation Glasscache, retaining the original "cache" concept while better conveying the central finding: that attackers are hiding behind the reflective, trusted "glass" of Google's brand and infrastructure.

3. Infection Vector: Abuse of Google-Hosted Infrastructure

Operation Glasscache — Boxstrike TeamTLP: WHITE

3.1 Google Sites as a distribution front

The defining characteristic of Operation Glasscache is the operators' near-exclusive reliance on Google Sites (sites.google.com) to host landing pages for their malware, rather than purpose-registered attacker-controlled domains. Boxstrike Team identified and directly reviewed multiple such pages, all following an identical template: a short pitch promising a game "cheat" or "mod menu", a download button routed through google.com/url?q= redirects or shortened links, and — in every case observed — a password-protected archive hosted on a third-party file locker (chiefly Mediafire or Google Drive itself).

3.2 Google Drive as a payload host

In addition to Sites, the operators use Google Drive directly as a file host for payload archives (see the Quantum lure above, which offers a drive.google.com/file/d/... link as a primary download option alongside Mediafire). Because Drive shared-file links resolve under Google's own domain and TLS certificate, they inherit the same reputation benefits as Sites pages and are similarly unlikely to be flagged by domain-reputation-based web filtering.

3.3 Why the attackers chose Google services

Boxstrike Team's assessment is that this is a deliberate, low-cost, high-yield infrastructure choice rather than an incidental one:

  • Trust and filtering bypass. google.com and its subdomains are near-universally allow-listed or given elevated trust scores by web filters, secure email gateways, and browser reputation engines. A malicious link on a corporate-owned or bulletproof-hosted domain is trivial to blocklist; a link on sites.google.com is not, because blocking the base domain would break access to millions of legitimate Sites pages.
  • Zero infrastructure cost and disposability. Google Sites and Drive accounts are free to create, require no domain registration or hosting payment (both of which can leave a financial paper trail), and are trivially disposable — when a page is reported and removed, the operators observed by Boxstrike Team simply stood up a near-identical replacement (e.g. spctra → spctrra) within a short window.
  • Valid, Google-issued TLS. Every payload delivery URL inherits a legitimate certificate under Google's own PKI, removing "invalid certificate" browser warnings that would otherwise deter less technical victims.
  • SEO and social distribution. Google Sites pages are indexable and shareable, and the observed lure pages are written to match exactly the kind of search queries ("free [game] cheat", "[game] mod menu download") that the target demographic — predominantly gamers seeking cheats — is likely to type into a search engine or follow from a Discord/YouTube link.
  • Layered redirection. Routing the actual payload through a secondary host (Mediafire) via a Google redirect (google.com/url?q=) adds a hop that both obscures the final destination from simple link-preview tools and splits the "reputation cost" of the delivery chain across two trusted-looking domains instead of one.

This combination allows financially motivated actors distributing commodity malware — who have no need for, and no access to, the kind of dedicated bulletproof infrastructure used by state-sponsored operators — to achieve a distribution reach and filter-evasion rate that would otherwise require considerably more investment.

4.1 Static analysis

The sample presents as a game "mod menu" but its embedded version resource tells a different story entirely: ProductName: "Temp File Mode Toggle", CompanyName: "GoldGrove Cloud Works, LLC" — a generic, deliberately unremarkable identity bearing no relation to the file's advertised purpose. This mismatch between external lure and internal metadata is a recurring signature of the operation.

4.1.1 Structural anomalies

  • Section table contains non-standard, corrupted-looking names — .n{Q, .3KC, .B'# — inconsistent with any standard compiler or linker output.
  • Only three imported functions are declared statically (GetProcessHeap, IsWindowVisible, GetStockObject), indicating the real API surface is resolved dynamically at runtime — a hallmark of custom packing/crypter layers.
  • The .B'# section, occupying roughly 95% of the file, measures 7.83–8.0 bits/byte of Shannon entropy across nearly its entire length — consistent with strong encryption or dense compression of the real payload.
  • The program entry point resolves into the .B'# section rather than .text; disassembly of the entry point and its call targets yields a single valid instruction followed by deliberately non-decodable byte sequences, consistent with anti-disassembly padding designed to defeat linear-sweep disassemblers.

4.1.2 Fraudulent code-signing certificate

The binary carries an Authenticode signature that fails chain validation: the certificate is self-signed (issuer and subject are identical), bearing the subject CN="Temp File Mode Toggle, O=GoldGrove Cloud Works, LLC, OU=Platform Operations, L=Toronto, S=ON, C=CA". Cross-referencing dynamic sandbox telemetry revealed a striking temporal anomaly: the certificate's "Not Before" date is 23 July 2026, while the PE's compile timestamp reads 15 April 2021 — a five-year impossibility indicating the certificate is freshly generated per build (or per campaign) by the packer/builder tooling, entirely decoupled from the (likely also forged) compile timestamp.

5.1 Static analysis

Unlike the ScarfaceStealer sample, Setup.exe is not itself packed or obfuscated at the PE level — it is a completely ordinary NSIS installer stub. Extracting its contents reveals a legitimate-looking Electron application shell (locale .pak files, Chromium DLLs, V8 snapshot blobs) padded to nearly 78 MB, inside which the malicious logic is concealed.

5.1.1 Brand impersonation and lack of signing

Version metadata claims CompanyName: "Razer Inc." with FileDescription: "Windows system configuration and optimization tool" — a generic description bearing no resemblance to any genuine Razer product, and the file carries no digital signature whatsoever (empty certificate directory), whereas authentic Razer software is consistently code-signed.

5.1.2 The ShinySpider NSIS wrapper

Among the NSIS plugin DLLs extracted from the installer was SpiderBanner.dll — not a standard or publicly documented NSIS plugin. This specific filename is documented in independent open-source reporting as a fingerprint of a custom NSIS wrapper attributed to a builder/packer identity referred to as "ShinySpider," used to package the "Silent Stealer" malware family, a Telegram-operated Malware-as-a-Service product.

5.1.3 The Electron payload: main.js

The Electron application's main.js (147 KB) is deliberately obfuscated using the javascript-obfuscator npm package — explicitly declared in the shipped package.json under an allowScripts block — using hex-named identifiers, string-array shuffling, and control-flow flattening. Despite the obfuscation, several literal strings survive dynamic string construction and are directly recoverable from the file:

https://api.telegram.org/bot
https://api.ipify.org
https://icanhazip.com
https://ifconfig.me/ip
https://ipinfo.io/ip

Alongside references to a CONFIG object containing TG_CHAT_ID / TG_BOT_TOKEN-style keys and a CONFIG['PAYLOADS'].forEach(...) loop, this confirms the malware uses the Telegram Bot API as its command-and-control and exfiltration channel, and is designed to download and stage further payloads rather than acting purely as a single-stage stealer.

5.3 Assessment

With MODERATE-TO-HIGH CONFIDENCE, Boxstrike Team assesses Setup.exe as a Silent Stealer deployment packaged by the ShinySpider builder. This assessment rests on the distinctive SpiderBanner.dll plugin fingerprint and a close behavioral match (Electron packaging, Defender-exclusion sequence, screen capture, Telegram C2) to independently published reporting on the same tooling. Boxstrike Team did not directly recover or crack the Telegram bot token or a fully decoded configuration, and this attribution should therefore be treated as a strong behavioral and infrastructural match rather than a code-level confirmation.

6. Why the Operators Chose This Approach

Operation Glasscache — Boxstrike TeamTLP: WHITE

Across both malware families, Boxstrike Team observes a consistent underlying logic that explains the specific technical choices made by the operators:

6.1 Target selection favors low-friction, high-volume victims

Both lures target people actively searching for free or cracked software — predominantly gamers seeking cheats, but also users of the generic "free software" pages. This population is (a) large, (b) accustomed to bypassing security warnings and disabling antivirus as a normal part of "installing a cheat," and (c) unlikely to report incidents to IT security teams, since the activity they were engaged in was itself against a game's terms of service. This makes social engineering around AV/Defender disabling far more effective than it would be against a corporate target.

6.2 Infrastructure economics over infrastructure sophistication

Neither family invests in bespoke C2 protocols or dedicated hosting. ScarfaceStealer relies on commodity packing to buy time against signature AV. Silent Stealer relies on the Telegram Bot API — a free, reliable, encrypted-transport messaging platform that most corporate web filters do not block, and that requires no infrastructure maintenance by the operator at all. Both choices minimize operational cost and maximize resilience to takedown, at the expense of the kind of stealth a targeted, well-resourced operator would build custom infrastructure to achieve.

6.3 Defender-exclusion abuse as a force multiplier

The explicit, repeated targeting of Windows Defender's exclusion list (both via GUI-instructed manual disabling on the Google Sites lure pages, and via automated Add-MpPreference calls inside Setup.exe) reflects a pragmatic reality: Windows Defender is the default, unpaid AV on the overwhelming majority of consumer Windows installations. Neutralizing it — rather than evading a wide range of possible third-party products — covers the largest practical share of the target population for the least engineering effort.

6.4 Reuse of Google's trust for both distribution and payload legitimacy signals

Beyond the distribution vector discussed in Section 3, using Google's infrastructure also lends a subtle legitimacy signal to the victim at the point of decision: a download button on a page under google.com, or a file served from drive.google.com, does not trigger the instinctive distrust that an unfamiliar domain would. This is social engineering that operates on the browser's own trust indicators (padlock icon, familiar domain) rather than on the content of the page itself.

8. YARA Rules

Operation Glasscache — Boxstrike TeamTLP: WHITE

The following rules were developed by Boxstrike Team against confirmed static indicators from the two fully analyzed samples in this report. Rule 1 and Rule 2 target the ScarfaceStealer / fake-metadata crypter layer; Rule 3 targets the ShinySpider NSIS wrapper used to package Silent Stealer.

rule Boxstrike_ScarfaceStealer_FakeCert_Crypter
{
    meta:
        description = "Fake 'Temp File Mode Toggle / GoldGrove Cloud Works' self-signed"
                       " certificate + packer combination used by ScarfaceStealer samples"
                       " distributed as game mod-menu lures (Operation Glasscache)"
        author       = "Boxstrike Team"
        date         = "2026-07-25"
        reference     = "d33fe738471c6f21f7f9c2c56893c5e286f110bb0eb242ec1b3f9145fccdaf79"

    strings:
        $meta_product  = "Temp File Mode Toggle" ascii wide
        $meta_company  = "GoldGrove Cloud Works, LLC" ascii wide

        $sec1 = ".n{Q"
        $sec2 = ".3KC"
        $sec3 = ".B'#"

        $api1 = "GetProcessHeap" ascii
        $api2 = "IsWindowVisible" ascii
        $api3 = "GetStockObject" ascii

    condition:
        uint16(0) == 0x5A4D
        and filesize > 5MB
        and (
            any of ($meta_*)
            or (2 of ($sec*) and 2 of ($api*))
        )
}

rule Boxstrike_HighEntropy_SingleSection_Packer
{
    meta:
        description = "Generic heuristic: single PE section occupying most of the file"
                       " with near-maximum entropy, consistent with the ScarfaceStealer"
                       " packing layer observed in Operation Glasscache"
        author       = "Boxstrike Team"

    condition:
        uint16(0) == 0x5A4D
        and pe.number_of_sections <= 8
        and for any i in (0..pe.number_of_sections - 1):
              ( pe.sections[i].raw_data_size > filesize * 0.7 )
}

{

9. Conclusion & Recommendations

Operation Glasscache — Boxstrike TeamTLP: WHITE

Operation Glasscache is not, in Boxstrike Team's assessment, the work of a single group in the way a nation-state APT cluster would be. It is better understood as a distribution pattern — the systematic abuse of Google Sites and Google Drive as free, trusted, disposable hosting for commodity malware — adopted independently or semi-independently by multiple financially motivated actors and MaaS customers, of whom ScarfaceStealer and Silent Stealer/ShinySpider operators are two clearly documented examples. The consistency of the lure format (game-cheat branding, password-protected archives, explicit AV-disabling instructions) across otherwise unrelated payload families suggests this pattern is itself being shared or templated within the underground cheat/crack distribution community.

Recommendations

  • Do not allow-list Google Sites or Drive wholesale. Where feasible, apply content-based or path-based scrutiny (e.g. newly created sites.google.com/view/* pages, or Drive files matching known-bad hashes) rather than trusting the base domain outright.
  • Alert on Defender configuration changes. Monitor for Add-MpPreference / Set-MpPreference PowerShell invocations and direct registry writes to Windows Defender\Exclusions, particularly when originating from a recently created or downloaded executable.
  • Treat "disable your antivirus to install this" instructions as a hard red flag in security-awareness training aimed at gaming and general consumer audiences, not only enterprise users.
  • Deploy the YARA rules in Section 8 across endpoint and email/web-gateway scanning to catch both documented families and near-variant rebuilds sharing the same packer or NSIS wrapper.
  • Monitor for Telegram Bot API traffic (api.telegram.org) originating from unexpected processes as a low-cost, high-value detection opportunity, since this channel is reused across many unrelated stealer families precisely because it is rarely blocked.
